Case III Recovery

Five rides that teach a night carrier approach one gate at a time — built from CV NATOPS, the Supercarrier operations guide, and the Heatblur manual, and graded on the things DCS itself does not check.

“i have zero idea how to do a case 3 recovery so i was flying around blind.” Casmo — the report this pack exists to answer
The instructional problem

Case III is not a skill. It is a procedure with gates.

A pilot busts a night recovery in one of four distinct places, and each one has a different cause. A single long mission teaches none of them: a bad marshal departure means you never reach the ball, and twenty minutes of flying buys you exactly one attempt at the part you actually got wrong.

So the syllabus is one ride per gate, each starting where the last one ended. You can fly the piece you keep failing, ten times, in the time one full recovery takes.

Case III is not a weather condition — it is mostly a clock. NATOPS requires it below a 1,000-foot ceiling or 5 nm visibility, and during all flight operations between half an hour after sunset and half an hour before sunrise. A clear, calm night is a Case III night. Single aircraft only.

The shape you are learning

CV-1 approach, marshal to ramp

Two numbers students get wrong from community guides and are corrected here. Platform is an altitude, not a range — 5,000 feet, wherever that falls. And the marshal radial hangs off the final bearing, the extension of the angled deck, which sits about 9–10° to port of BRC; computing it off BRC puts you off the arc before you start.

The syllabus

Five rides, each starting where the last one ended

1

The Stack

CoachedAirborne on the fix~10 min

Holding, and only holding. Left-hand, six-minute racetrack, inbound leg over the fix, and the one thing that actually takes practice: stretching and shortening the legs so you depart at the assigned second.

Graded on Departure within ±10 seconds of the approach time · altitude held · pattern flown on the correct side

2

The Push

CoachedOn the fix at push time~8 min

The descent. 250 knots, 4,000 feet per minute, and the rate change at platform that half of all students miss because they are hunting for a DME instead of watching an altimeter.

Graded on Rate of descent below platform · level at 1,200 ft by 10 DME · the platform call made at 5,000 ft

3

The Approach

CoachedLevel 1,200 ft, 12 DME~7 min

Correcting to the final bearing, dirtying up at 8, on speed by 6, and holding 1,200 feet until the glideslope comes to you rather than diving to meet it. Soft corrections — the reason NATOPS mandates soft headings on final is to stop students chasing needles.

Graded on Configured by 8 DME · 150 knots at 6 DME · altitude held to intercept · lineup inside 1.7°

4

The Ball

Coached3 DME on glideslope~6 min, repeatable

The last mile, over and over. Ball call in the right format, then flying the ball instead of spotting the deck. Bolter every time on the first pass by design, so the bolter pattern gets taught rather than discovered: straight ahead to 1,200 feet, level turns, abeam with your state, final at 4 DME.

Graded on Ball call format · glidepath and lineup against the LSO's own thresholds · a bolter pattern flown level

5

Night CQ

Check rideAirborne, 60 nm, weather~25 min

Everything, once, for real. Night, low ceiling, no gates drawn, no cue cards, no coaching. Check in with Marshal at 50 miles and take yourself to the deck.

Graded on All of the above, in one pass

Rides 1–4 carry every aid: helper gates drawn along the profile, a cue card at each decision, and the boat card on the kneeboard. Ride 5 has none of them. That is the same split the White Knights pack uses — coached means every aid on, and exactly one ride is the check.

What the sim actually does

This is what decides the design

Supercarrier gives us

  • The marshal assignment, with the correct angels + 15 rule
  • An expected approach time, radar contact, the handoff to approach
  • The platform acknowledgement and “fly bullseye”
  • ACLS lock and “say needles”
  • “…three-quarter mile, call the ball”, and Paddles' voice all the way down
  • IFLOLS, cut lights, waveoff lights, and published deviation thresholds to grade against

And does not

  • Enforce the approach time — you can push half an hour early and nothing happens
  • Model Delta, emergency marshal, or the 12-mile arc
  • Fly a Case III with AI aircraft — they revert to Case I in the pattern, so the stack is not populated and pretended otherwise

Which is the whole reason these rides carry their own triggers. We grade the things DCS ignores — departure against the approach time, rate of descent below platform, altitude at 10, configuration at 8, speed at 6 — and let Paddles grade the part Paddles is good at.

On the radio

The sequence, in order

  1. Marshal, [side number], marking mother's [bearing] for [range], angels [alt], low state [fuel].You, inbound, inside 50 nm
  2. Case III recovery, CV-1 approach, expected BRC [heading], altimeter [setting]. Marshal mother's [radial] radial, [distance] DME, angels [alt]. Expected approach time is [time].Marshal — write it down, all of it
  3. [side number], established angels [alt]. State [fuel].You, in the stack, on altitude
  4. [side number] commencing, [altimeter], state [fuel].You, at the approach time — not before
  5. [side number], checking in, [distance] miles, [fuel].You, to Approach after the handoff
  6. [side number], platform.You, passing 5,000 feet
  7. [side number], ACLS lock on [distance] miles, say needles.Approach, around 6 miles — answer up/down, left/right
  8. [side number], [glidepath], [lineup], three-quarter mile, call the ball.Approach, handing you to Paddles
  9. [side number], [type], ball, [fuel state].You. “Clara” if you cannot see it, and then Paddles talks you down.

Fuel state is spoken in thousands of pounds — “three point five”. Altitude in angels — “one point two”. Heading corrections on final are soft — “three zero one, right five”.

Before you push

The cockpit half, which is where the two jets differ

F-14A / F-14B

TACAN
T/R. Not A/A — that is for tankers. BCN is not functional in DCS.
ICLS
The AN/ARA-63 panel: channel set, POWER ON. The Tomcat does have ICLS — the ARA-63 is the airborne half of it, 20 channels.
ACLS
APN-154 MODE → ACLS, or the SPN-42 never locks. Link 4A on, MODE TAC.
Steering
AWL/PCD submode; HUD and VDI AWL switches to ILS for crossed pointers (full scale 2° HUD, 1.5° VDI).
Coupling
ACL READY and A/P CPLR lit, then press nosewheel steering. Disengage on PLM. Pair with APC for a hands-off approach.
On speed
15 units AOA — the amber donut.
Watch for
VOICE means ACL is gone, go voice. TILT means the datalink dropped and the AFCS is about to.

Heatblur's own note, worth teaching: the ARA-63 glideslope is unreliable until you are within 20° of the final bearing. Turning the wrong way on intercept makes it climb away from you and track inverted.

F/A-18C

TACAN
T/R.
ICLS
ILS on the UFC, channel on the scratchpad. Bars appear next to the velocity vector.
ACLS
Mode I coupled approach — one press of the coupled autopilot, expect CPLD P/R.
The catch
Mode I controls pitch and roll only. Add ATC in approach mode, which holds AOA — not airspeed — for the throttle half.
On speed
8.1 units — the middle tick of the AOA bracket.
Requires
ACLS needs the Supercarrier module. It is not available off the free boat.

The kneeboard boat card in every ride carries the hull's TACAN channel and ident, the ICLS channel, Link 4, ACLS state and the BRC — the numbers you dial before you push, now printed in the brief as well as the kneeboard.

What the rides are aimed at

The eight documented busts

Radial off the wrong referenceComputing marshal from BRC instead of the final bearing. Nine or ten degrees, and you start the approach off the arc.
Hunting for platform on the DMEIt is 5,000 feet. Students looking for a range gate fly straight through the rate change.
Faster than 2,000 fpm below platformThe single most common profile bust, and the one that puts you low and fast at 10.
Not level at 1,200 by 10 DMEWhich leaves no time to configure by 8 and no chance of being on speed by 6.
Dirtying up lateArriving at 6 fast and high, then trying to fix both at once inside the final.
Chasing the needlesLarge corrections on final. The phraseology is soft on purpose — “right five”, not “turn right”.
Spotting the deckLooking through the ball at the landing area and diving for it. The classic night killer.
Turning while climbing on the bolterAll waveoff and bolter pattern turns are level. Every one.

Two corrections made during research and worth recording: the F-14 does carry ICLS (the AN/ARA-63 is its airborne receiver), and the carrier TACAN is used in T/R, not A/A — the A/A folklore most likely comes from mission builders picking the “TACAN A/A” beacon type in the editor, which leaves the channel looking right and gives a receiving aircraft nothing.
