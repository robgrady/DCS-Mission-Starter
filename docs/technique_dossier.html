How the campaign carries state: it doesn't — it narrates it

There is no mechanism. No flag, warehouse value, or score is read from a previous mission; none of the five files declares a campaign stage. Continuity is done entirely in prose and casting: M2's brief opens with "the morning landing at Autobahn Strip Alpha was executed successfully", M3's opens with the diplomatic fallout of M2, M5's with the defection from M3 and a retaliation for a radar site you never saw attacked. The one thing that recurs mechanically is a character: MiG-21 pilot 805 is a unit in M2, M3 and (by reference) M5, and his lines assume you remember him.

Read for Bet 5. The best campaign in this set is a fixed, linear story with the illusion of consequence. The consequence a player feels — "I escorted him, now they've recalled the ambassadors" — costs the author one paragraph. A campaign engine that merely automates what Fulda does (next brief references last result) already matches the state of the art; anything that carries a real variable forward (the wingman who leaked fuel in M5 is not on the M6 line-up; the channel plan doesn't silently change) is beyond it.

1b. Reflected — Red Flag 81-2, Mission 2 (SEAD)Nellis, 27 Jan 1981, 13:49. Rocket 11, F-4E with two AGM-45A and an ALQ-131, part of a nine-flight package. 40 MB, 398 files, 327 voice clips, 34 kneeboard pages.

The logic is not in the file. The .miz has an empty trigrules, empty triggers, zero zones and zero Lua. An ext_loader entry points at Mods/campaigns/F-4E Red Flag 81-2/F-4E-RED-FLAG-81-2: this is Eagle Dynamics' protected-campaign format, and the trigger tree lives in that encrypted library. So there is no trigger teardown here. What the file still exposes, and what this section is built from, is everything else: every unit, every route with its locked times, every radio preset, every kneeboard, and the entire dialogue script (4 043 dictionary entries, 466 subtitles) in authoring order.

How the package is built

Nine flights are on the radio; five are in the file. Rocket (4× F-4E Weasel), Demon 1 and 2 (8× F-15C), Monkey (4× Tornado GR4 strikers) and Pepsi (4× A-10C) exist as units. Bluenose, Cougar, Hate and Rattler exist only as voice: they taxi, check in with Magic, call winchester and break over the field entirely in audio, with nothing flying. The player never sees them and never needs to; the package sounds twice its size for the cost of a few hundred clips. Against them: two four-ship F-5E aggressor groups plus eight single-ship F-5Es named MIG 1–4 and IVAN 1–4, two red C-130s, an SA-2 site, a Fan Song, Shilkas, KS-19s, and a 206-static target complex built from containers, T-walls and watchtowers.

Every wingman and every Eagle is its own single-unit group, uncontrolled, started by the hidden logic. That is what lets Showtime kill "Rocket 12" by name and re-spawn Demons as "alive" after regeneration: a four-ship in one group can't be addressed one jet at a time. The player's HOLD waypoint carries a locked ETA of +46 minutes — 14:35, the push time on the mission data card — so the route itself holds the timeline.

Read. This is the honesty bar. Reflected's flight plan card is a mechanically-derived document — it reads as if the route was computed and then written down — and the ext_loader means the only thing a reader can ever verify is the paperwork against the world. That is precisely what Bet 1 does, and it would have passed this mission with one finding. The gaps in Fulda were authorial; the near-absence of gaps here is procedural. The product's job is to make the second kind free.

1c. Baltic Dragon / Whipbird — The Rampagers, Mission 5Syria, 7 Jun 2016, 04:05. Ram 2-1, F/A-18C off USS Truman: Case III launch, four countries' airspace, a tanker, a border ROZ with a Joint Strike Cell. 40 MB, 643 files, 514 voice clips, 34 kneeboard pages, 71 Lua scripts. Plus the campaign guide, the checklist book, and Mission 1's briefing and kneeboard.

Protected, but the scripts are in the clear. Same ext_loader format as Reflected — empty trigger tables, encrypted library in the campaign folder. The difference is that this campaign does its work in Lua, and the 71 script files sit unencrypted in l10n/DEFAULT. The trigger tree only decides when each script runs; the scripts are the mission. So this one is readable after all.

What the scripts do

Every voice line is assembled at runtime rather than recorded whole. The jet's painted board number is read from its draw arguments (442, 31, 32) and spoken digit by digit from per-speaker sound banks — playernos/, mothernos/, wingmannos/, departure/ — with "Start" and "End" variants of each digit so the last one falls. Fuel state comes from unit:getFuel() and altitude from getPoint().y, both spoken as numbers, so "Two, state 7.2" is true when it's said. A single flag (2) is the radio's busy line: every script sets it, queues its clips with per-clip delays through timer.scheduleFunction, and clears it at the end, so nothing steps on anything.

The rules in the brief are enforced and scored. Departure-Altitude/Heading/Speed.lua each fire a Departure correction ("remain below 500 feet until 7 DME. Correct immediately") and set the departure flag; the restricted area escalates through four voices (wingman → Image → Magic → Strike); a weapons-event handler turns S_EVENT_SHOT into Pickle / Dart / Rifle / Guns by weapon category, debounced. Mission-Debrief.lua prints the scorecard at shutdown: base 50, targets itemised, departure 5, airspace 5, ROE 20, carrier recovery 10 or divert 5, and one flag (123, escalation with Syrian or Russian forces) that overrides everything with "mission failure". The windshield switch is bound as a push-to-check-in shortcut through a_start_listen_command called from mission-scope Lua — the guide's "Radio Options Shortcut", and the only place I've seen a cockpit switch wired to an F10 item.

Fifty-four warehouses are limited. This is the only mission in the set modelling attrition.

The paperwork, checked against the file

The Mission 5 comms card lists forty presets across both radios and four TACANs. All forty match the jet (COMM 1: 137.4, 127.5, 265, 128.75, 261.5, 255, 262 … 260, 263; COMM 2: 305, 301.5 … 251.05, 260, 263). Texaco 51X, Shell 61X, Arco 71X and Mother 75X are the beacons in the file. Zero findings in the mission. The findings are in the documents: Mission 1's briefing says "Departure: CASE I at 0630" and its kneeboard overview says "Departure: 1230 (CASE I)" with a 0730–0830 recovery; the same brief names the player's element "1-3 / 1-4" in the text and "Ram-2-1 / Ram-2-2" on the overview card. Two edits apart, in two documents that ship together.

Read. Rampagers is the highest bar in the set for the thing Sortie Starter is actually about: the paper matches the world because the world is generated to match the paper, the rules on the card are the rules the scripts enforce, and the debrief tells you which ones you broke. Its remaining gaps are exactly where a human copied a number between two documents by hand — the one step our generator doesn't have.

Five things the scan settles

Every commercial mission is protected. 97 of 97 carry ext_loader and an empty trigger table. Fulda, which sold on the same store, is the only trigger-readable expert work we have, and it stays the teardown reference. The commercial craft is readable only where an author left Lua in the clear — which Rampagers does, at scale: 1 000 script files, 594 distinct by content, and a Temp-* library of eight modules copied into all seventeen missions (restricted-area warning, declare emergency, activate north/south/global, debrief, wave-off). That is a campaign framework, parameterised per mission, and it is the closest thing in the corpus to what our generator is.

Reflected carries state by hand, mission to mission, inside one sortie. MIG Killers is thirteen events, each split into Departure → exercise(s) → RTB as separate files; the .cmp only lists the Departure file, so the chain is loaded by the hidden logic. Along each chain the author advanced the clock and burned the fuel himself:

This is mechanical state — clock, fuel, stores — done manually, 45 times, with two slips. It is the exact thing a generator does for nothing, and the exact thing our ride model already is.

Branching exists in the platform and is used once in 97 missions. All four campaign files are linear, one mission per stage, until Raven One's last stage, where the next file is chosen by the previous mission's score: 0–50 or 53–100 loads the credits, 51 loads "M15 ALT", 52 loads "M15 ALT NO WEED". The score is the state channel and the stage is the switch. Nothing in the platform stops a generator from doing that at every stage.

The say/do reader needs eyes. Reflected's in-game briefing is one line: "refer to the briefing PDF". The numbers live on kneeboard images and in the Doc folder, so the automatic checks came back "n/a" on 60 of 97 missions — a requirement for Bet 1 (read the kneeboard pages, not just the text), not a clean bill. Where text existed the checks found: a 145.25 MHz frequency briefed in four Raven One missions that no radio in the file holds; two briefed dates that don't match the file (Raven One M08, Rampagers M02 — likely narrative dates, which is itself a lesson for the reader); Rampagers M11 briefing a 0630 start on a 04:30 mission. And 75 of 97 missions fly a stock DCS callsign (Dodge, Roman, Enfield) under a voiced one (Rocket, Raven, Ram) — a platform limit every author lives with, and one our brief should state instead of hide.

Nobody forces immortality. Fulda did; none of the four commercial campaigns do (Rampagers and Red Flag force easy comms off, nothing else). The professionals protect the story with a regeneration zone and a scorecard, not a god switch.

The frequency gate triplet

Every handoff is three triggers: set by Jester (WSO tunes it if he holds the radios), NOT set (a reminder after 25–30 s), and set → next flag. The story does not advance until the radio is right. This is the exact fix for "it says channel 2 but I had to tune it by hand": the mission checks the tune, it doesn't assume it.

c_cockpit_param_equal_to COMM_FREQ / AUX_FREQ · Fulda M1–M6, 13 gates per mission Plan · Case III readback check

Jester-has-radios branch

A cockpit switch decides who tunes. If the player has handed the radios to Jester, the script drives the Jester wheel to tune; if not, it prompts the player. Twelve "clone la freq du player" triggers keep the WSO's set in step with the pilot's. The mission respects a player choice instead of overriding it.

Engine RPM (args 299/300 > 0.55), generators, gear handle (arg 5 < 0.3 = airborne), IFF drum digits, ladder, chocks. "Start wrong engine" and "start without clearance" nags. This is what makes a cold start a lesson rather than a wait.

3. Reflected's Red Flag 81-2 inflight guideA real document used with a real campaign. This is the benchmark for what our kneeboard should carry, page for page.

Twenty pages, and every one of them is something a pilot needs at a moment when the briefing screen is gone. Mission 2 of the campaign carries all twenty on the kneeboard, plus nine mission cards (data card, flight plan, maps) and five checklist pages: 34 pages in the jet. Rampagers does the same with a 30-page NATOPS-style checklist book (normal, combat, Case I/III departure and recovery, catapult trim and weight tables, ACL approach) plus five mission cards — its comms card adds a home/divert table with elevation, TACAN, ILS and BRC, and a tanker table with altitude, TACAN and track heading. Read. The guide is not a brief. It is the paper you'd want at 300 knots with a dead radio, and the pages that separate it from our output are the boring ones: parking, divert, NORDO, the airfield diagram. Three of those four are data we already have in pydcs and are not printing.

4. What it means for the three destinations

  • Better author, nowFrequency-gate triplet in the Case III pack (c_cockpit_param_equal_to), cockpit-state gates for dirty-up and hook, random cue variants, a descent staircase for the approach coach, entry–what–exit trigger names, and a termination guarantee in every coach so no ride can fail to end. All are trigger-only; none breaks the no-Lua rule.
  • Bet 1 — the re-brief readerThe ledger above is the spec. Checks that would have caught every gap in expert work: briefed frequencies vs presets and vs transmission frequencies; briefed loadout vs pylons; briefed times vs the mission clock; briefed date vs file date; briefed squawk vs IFF argument ranges; callsign in text vs callsign in file; and, across a set, preset drift between missions. The dissect script already reads all of these; the reader is the same code with a verdict column.
  • Bet 5 — the campaign engineThe mechanism is in the platform: score-keyed alternates per stage (Raven One, once) and chained files with carried clock, fuel and stores (MIG Killers, by hand). Level one is what Fulda does: the next brief references the last result. Level two is generating the alternates Baltic Dragon built once and the state Reflected carried 45 times. Level three is the honesty check across the whole campaign, so channel plans and clocks can't drift between our own missions — the two slips in the MIG Killers chain are the proof it's needed.
  • The folder, not the fileEvery campaign on the shelf ships as .cmp + missions + a Doc folder. Sortie Starter should export exactly that: campaign definition, chained or branched missions, printed briefing and kneeboard PDFs and an inflight guide per map — all derived from the recipe, so none of it can disagree with the world.
