The reframe

What changed, and why the roadmap should change with it

When the competitive read was written, the training tracks were a category inside a mission generator. Two things have happened since.

The syllabus became a factory. Case III was built on the same bones as the White Knights rides — a doctrine module, a route module, a coach module, a flag block — and it took one release instead of the arc that produced the first one. The marginal cost of the next syllabus is now low and known. That is a different strategic asset from "we have some training missions."

You got an instrument. Google Analytics landed this release, and the roadmap's whole "Later" tier is gated on demand data that did not exist until now. Those items were not deprioritised; they were unmeasurable. They are measurable on Monday.

So the question is no longer "what else could a mission generator do." It is: what makes a flight school compound, and what brings students to the door.

Six bets, in dependency order

What to build

1
The wedgeAcquisitionMachinery exists

Re-brief any mission

Upload someone else's .miz — BriefingRoom's, Liberation's, a purchased campaign, a squadron's — and get your paperwork back for it.

A Flightline kneeboard set, a real brief, a flight-plan card, a DTC cartridge where the airframe takes one. And the part nobody else in this space can ship: a say/do report — every claim the existing brief makes that the mission does not actually contain.

This converts users of the incumbent without asking them to stop using it, which is the cheapest acquisition there is. It also turns a year of invisible discipline into a visible product, and the demo sells itself: run it on a competitor's mission and the output is the pitch.

Payoff
Why it is cheapYou already read .miz through pydcs, and every generator it needs is shipped. This is a new front door onto existing machinery plus an inference layer for missions you did not author.
2
RetentionData exists

The logbook

You grade pilots inside the mission, from triggers alone, and then throw the result away when they close DCS.

A signed record — rides flown, tracks completed, qualifications earned, dates — printed in the same publication language as the kneeboards. Squadrons will use it for currency tracking, which is a use case nobody in DCS tooling serves.

It is the reason to come back on day thirty, and it is what makes an eleven-ride track compound instead of being eleven missions somebody flies once. Without it, every syllabus you build is a consumable.

Effort
Payoff
The hard partNot the page — the capture. A grade lives in a running mission; getting it back out without a server, an account or a Lua hook is the design problem. Solve it once and it serves every track you ever ship.
3
CompoundingThe factory

The next three syllabi — and then let other people write them

Two engines proved the shape. The question now is what the third teaches, and whether you have to be the one building it.

Candidates, in the order the airframe population argues for: BFM (the most-wanted skill in the sim and the one with the clearest gates), SEAD/Wild Weasel (a real procedure with real numbers, and nobody teaches it), low-level navigation (the White Knights rides already contain half of it), and a rotary track once helicopters land.

Then the multiplier: the .sspack format and the admin upload already exist. A published pack format plus an authoring guide turns a squadron's own training documents into content you did not have to write. That is the difference between a catalog and a platform.

Effort
Payoff
The risk to nameUser-authored content carries a moderation and quality cost, and a bad pack under your banner damages the one thing you sell. Signed packs and a review step are the price of this door.
4
First nightResearch paid for

Situations, and a front line

The only place where adding a control makes this product simpler instead of more complex.

A situation — Russo-Georgian War, Desert Storm, night one — sets coalition, map, era and posture in one pick. You already own every component: theater identity, historical airspace, air corridors, the Iraq research. You have simply never named the combinations.

Pair it with a single front-line vector per situation. Nothing in the engine currently knows where the war is, which is why the Cold War and GWOT eras read as an open theatre with things scattered in it. One vector improves target placement, threat gradient, corridor relevance and briefing language at once — and it makes the situations feel like wars rather than settings.

Effort
Payoff
Do them togetherThe front line is cheap on its own and transformative underneath situations. Shipping situations first without it produces named settings that still feel like sandboxes.
5
PositioningGap they named

Light persistent campaigns

BriefingRoom says "not dynamic — look at Liberation." Liberation is thirty minutes of strategy layer per turn. Nothing sits in the middle, and the middle is where most people live.

Six to ten missions, a front that moves, losses that stick, a story that finishes in a fortnight. The primitive is already there — a track is an ordered set of missions that travel together — and the state can live in a small file the user carries, on the same recipes-not-artifacts principle as share links. No server, no account.

Sequenced fifth deliberately: it wants the front line from bet 4 and the persistence design from bet 2. Built after those, it is mostly assembly. Built before them, it is a new subsystem.

Payoff
DependencyWants bet 4's front line and bet 2's carried-state design. Doing it third would mean inventing both badly.
6
Deferred, with a trigger

Multiplayer and squadron features

A real gap, and still the wrong thing to close now.

Dynamic spawning, cargo scoping, hot starts, server ops — a different customer with different support costs, against a win condition that is currently the first-night single-player experience.

The trigger to revisit is now measurable, which it was not last month: squadron-shaped usage in the analytics — the same recipe generated repeatedly from different browsers, or whole-track downloads clustering. Write the trigger down now so the decision is made by evidence rather than by whoever asks loudest.

The instrument you just installed

Four questions the analytics should answer first

The roadmap's "Later" tier has said demand-gated — the analytics tab decides for months, against data that did not exist. It does now. These are the questions worth asking of the first month of it, and what each one actually gates.

Which of the three doors do people actually use? Fly Now, Library and Builder are now tagged separately on every generate. If Fly Now dominates, the Builder's remaining polish is not the work — Quick Flight completion is. If the Library dominates, bets 1–3 are the whole roadmap.
Do people who open a track ever finish it? Track opens versus ride generates versus repeat generates of the same ride. A track people open and abandon at ride two is a packaging problem, not a content problem — and it decides whether bet 3 is "three more syllabi" or "fix the first one."
Does anybody come back? Returning visitors against first-time. This is the single number that says whether the logbook (bet 2) is a retention feature or a retention rescue, and it changes its priority rather than its design.
Where do people leave? View changes without a following generate. A Builder full of people who never press the button is a different product problem from a Builder nobody opens, and the two are currently indistinguishable.
Discipline

What not to build, and why saying so is the strategy

BriefingRoom's "Quick" generator asks for roughly a hundred decisions before it will give you anything. That is a way of declining to have an opinion. Your opinions are the asset, and every item below is a way of losing them.

The Full GeneratorFeature-toggle parity with the incumbent. Chasing their knob count costs the only thing they cannot copy cheaply, which is that your defaults are defensible.
A waypoint editorAlready decided and worth keeping decided. Where a mission needs a plan, the mission brings it — the pilot is never handed a flight plan he did not ask for.
A kneeboard editorThey shipped one because their default output is not good enough. Yours is the product. Editing it is a confession.
A desktop appTheir own site tells users the desktop build works better than the web one. Web reliability is a competitive advantage — market it rather than diluting it.
AccountsEvery persistence feature here is designed to work without one: recipes not artifacts, state the user carries. An account is a support burden, a privacy liability and a signup wall in front of a free tool.
LocalisationNine languages is real reach and it multiplies the cost of every document you generate — and the documents are the differentiator. Revisit when the documents stop changing.
Lua in the .mizThe no-script guarantee is why missions load everywhere, survive DCS updates, and can be trusted by squadrons with server rules. It has forced better designs twice; keep it.

If you only do one thing

Bet 1, and then read the numbers

Re-briefing an arbitrary .miz is the only item on this page that brings people in rather than serving the people already here. It monetises a year of discipline that is currently invisible to anyone who has not flown your missions, and it costs mostly plumbing.

Everything after it is a retention story, and a retention story is worth building once there is somebody to retain.

The one thing this roadmap deliberately does not do is add configurability. There is a version of this product that answers BriefingRoom control for control, and it is a worse product than the one you have — because the moment your defaults stop being defensible, the say/do rule, the sourced numbers and the 3,780 tests stop being worth what they cost.
