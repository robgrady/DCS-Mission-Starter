# Aviation basics — the six numbers you fly by

*Fifteen minutes. This is the ground school a study simulator never gives you: not how to work the cockpit, but what the instruments are telling you and why a pilot cares.*

## 1. Airspeed — which one?

The airspeed indicator reads the pressure of the air hitting the pitot tube, and that number — **indicated airspeed** — is the one the airplane feels. Stall, flap limits, approach speed and the rudder-not-aileron rule are all indicated speeds, and they do not change with altitude. **True airspeed** is how fast you are moving through the air; at 20,000 feet the air is thin, so the same indicated speed is a much higher true speed. **Groundspeed** is true airspeed plus or minus the wind, and it is the only one that gets you to the target on time. The flight-plan card in this product prints groundspeed for exactly that reason. **Mach** is speed as a fraction of the speed of sound; it matters for the jet's limits at altitude and for intercept geometry, not for the pattern.

## 2. Altitude — above what?

Set the altimeter to the field's pressure (QNH) and it reads height above sea level. That is the number on the chart, the number ATC uses, and the number every altitude on a Sortie Starter brief means unless it says otherwise. Height above the ground — what the radar altimeter shows — is a different thing, and in a low-level ride it is the one that keeps you alive. Above a transition altitude everyone sets the standard 29.92 inches (1013 hPa) and calls the result a flight level, so that two airplanes at "FL250" are really at the same height whatever the local pressure is doing. A brief prints the QNH; set it before you taxi.

## 3. Angle of attack — the number the wing cares about

The wing does not stall at a speed; it stalls at an angle between the wing and the air it is meeting. Weight, bank and g change the speed at which that angle is reached, which is why an airplane can stall at any speed. Jets built for the carrier and for fighting — the Phantom included — show angle of attack in the cockpit in "units", and the airplane is flown on it: approach at one number, best turn at another, never past a third. When the F-4E brief says "fifteen units", it means an angle, and the airspeed it happens at depends on how heavy you are and how hard you are pulling.

## 4. Energy — the account you spend in a turn

Speed and altitude are the same thing in different forms, and a turn spends both. Pull hard and the airspeed falls; hold the pull and the altitude follows. An airplane's sustained turn is the rate it can hold without losing either; anything harder is borrowed and must be paid back by unloading, adding power, or trading height. The formation stage on climbs, descents and speed, and *Turning the Phantom*, are both lessons in this account. The pilot who wins a fight is usually the one who spent less of it getting there.

## 5. Heading and course — true, magnetic, and the wind

The compass reads magnetic north; a chart is drawn to true north; the difference is variation, and it changes with where you are. Sortie Starter prints true headings and says so on every card, because the simulator does not publish a variation it can verify. Course is the line you want over the ground; heading is where the nose points to fly it, which in a crosswind is not the same. The timing track will teach you both in the only way that sticks.

## 6. Time — the number that makes a package work

A single airplane can be early or late and nobody notices. Four airplanes and a SEAD flight cannot, because the whole point of a package is that the missiles are off before the strikers are in the ring and everyone is out before the frag comes down. So every professional flight plan carries a clock: a time the plan is anchored on — takeoff, a push point, or the time on target — and an ETA at every waypoint solved from it. The stopwatch is a flight instrument. Hack it at wheels-up.

## The radio, briefly

The frequencies on the comms card are the ones the mission actually holds; nothing is on a card that nothing in the file can answer on. Channels are presets in your radio, where the aircraft has them. Guard (243.0 UHF / 121.5 VHF) is for emergencies and nothing else. Say who you are calling, who you are, where you are, and what you want, in that order, and stop transmitting.

## The pattern

Upwind, crosswind, downwind, base, final. The military overhead adds a break over the runway at pattern altitude, a turn to downwind, and a descending turn onto final — the ride on the tactical overhead teaches it as the 70th flew it. The rule that matters in every version: configure early, fly the numbers on the brief, and if it is not right by short final, go around. A go-around is a procedure, not a failure.
