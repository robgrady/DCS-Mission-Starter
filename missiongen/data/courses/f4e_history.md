# The Phantom: where it came from, what it taught

*Chapter 0 of the F-4E FRS. Read it before the contact phase. Nothing in the airplane's handling or its weapons makes sense until you know what it was built to be — and what it was not.*

## An interceptor the Navy did not order

The F-4 began as a McDonnell proposal nobody asked for. In 1953 the St. Louis company, having lost the Navy's day-fighter competition to Vought's Crusader, kept working on a twin-engined derivative of its F3H Demon on its own money. The Navy first bought it as a single-seat attack airplane, then in 1955 rewrote the requirement into something new: a two-seat, all-weather fleet-defense interceptor whose entire job was to sit on station a long way from the carrier, find raiders with its own radar, and kill them with radar-guided Sparrow missiles before they reached the ships. Two J79 engines, a second crewman to run the radar, eleven external stations, and — because the missile was the weapon and a gun was thought to be dead weight — no cannon.

The XF4H-1 first flew on 27 May 1958 and beat Vought's F8U-3 in a fly-off the following year; the second seat and the second engine were the deciding arguments. Before it reached a squadron it had set an absolute altitude record above 98,000 feet, a speed record above Mach 2.5, and a low-altitude speed record that stood for decades. The first fleet squadrons stood up in 1961.

## Why the Air Force bought a Navy fighter

In 1961 the USAF flew the F4H against its own F-106 and found the Navy airplane carried more, saw further and turned better. It ordered the type as the F-110 Spectre; the 1962 tri-service designation system made it the F-4C. Air Force Phantoms gained a full set of controls in the rear cockpit, a boom receptacle instead of a probe, and inertial navigation, and the F-4D of 1965 added the avionics for the air-to-ground work the Air Force actually wanted it for. By the middle of the decade the same airframe was the front-line fighter of the Navy, the Marine Corps and the Air Force — the only time that has happened.

## Vietnam, and the missile that did not work

The Phantom went to war in 1965 as the interceptor it was designed to be, and found a war it was not designed for. Over North Vietnam the rules of engagement demanded visual identification, which meant a merge, which meant a turning fight with a MiG-17 or MiG-21 at ranges where the Sparrow could not be used and the Sidewinder often would not track. Studies of the air war — the Navy's Ault report and the Air Force's Red Baron reports — put the probability of kill for a Sparrow launched in combat at roughly one in ten, and for the early Sidewinder not much better. Crews had been trained to fire missiles, not to fight; most had never seen a MiG-17 turn.

Two responses came out of that. The Navy's was the Fighter Weapons School at Miramar in 1969 — Topgun — which taught air combat maneuvering against dissimilar aircraft and produced the Navy's improved 1972 kill ratio. The Air Force's was slower and more mechanical: gun pods on the centerline first, then the F-4E.

## The E: a gun, a new radar, and a seventh fuel cell

The F-4E first flew on 30 June 1967. It carried an internal 20 mm M61A1 Vulcan cannon in a lengthened nose, with 639 rounds; the smaller APQ-120 radar that fitted around it; J79-GE-17 engines; and a seventh fuselage fuel cell to make up for the volume the gun took. More than 1,300 were built, and it became the most numerous Phantom variant and the one most air forces flew. From 1972 the wing gained leading-edge maneuvering slats — the hard-wing Phantom's tendency to depart controlled flight at high angle of attack was killing crews in training as well as in combat, and the slats moved the edge of the envelope outward by a wide margin at the cost of a little top-end speed. Later blocks added TISEO, a telescopic camera on the left wing for long-range visual identification. Heatblur's F-4E is a slatted Block 45 aircraft: the gun, the slats and the APQ-120 are the airplane you fly.

In the Linebacker campaigns of 1972 the F-4E and F-4D produced the Air Force's aces — Ritchie, DeBellevue and Feinstein — and the Navy's F-4J produced Cunningham and Driscoll. The gun accounted for a small share of the kills; what mattered more was that crews were finally being trained to fight, and given radar and identification aids that let them use the Sparrow at the ranges it was built for.

## Israel, 1973: the SA-6 and the low-level attack

Israel received the F-4E — *Kurnass*, the sledgehammer — from 1969 and used it as its heavy striker. In the Yom Kippur War of October 1973 the Phantom squadrons met the SA-6 and the ZSU-23-4 for the first time in numbers, and lost airplanes at a rate that changed how everybody flew. The answer was to go under the radar SAMs and accept the guns: low-level ingress, a pop-up to a short dive, one pass, and formation attacks designed so that four aircraft were exposed for seconds rather than minutes. The B'NAI attack you will fly in the weapons phase, and the double-90 and echelon attacks beside it, come from that experience by way of the 70th Tactical Fighter Squadron's own 1980 planning sheets.

## Wild Weasel and the long tail

The F-4G Wild Weasel of 1978 turned the Phantom into the airplane that hunted the SAMs that had hunted it, and it was still doing so over Iraq in 1991 as the last USAF Phantom in front-line service. The US Air Force retired its last Weasels in 1996; QF-4 target drones flew until 2016. Abroad the type served far longer: Greece, Turkey, South Korea and Iran flew upgraded F-4Es into the 2020s. Of 5,195 Phantoms built — the largest production run of any Western supersonic fighter — the E was the model that defined it.

## What the airplane will teach you

**It was built to be flown by two people.** The WSO is not a passenger. In DCS that seat is Jester or a human, and the airplane rewards a crew that briefs who does what before the engines start.

**Use the rudder above fifteen units of angle of attack.** Aileron at high alpha produces adverse yaw, and adverse yaw in a hard-wing Phantom is how a departure begins. The slats make the E more forgiving than the C and D were; they do not repeal the rule. The White Knights checkout will say this to you several times.

**Speed is life, and the F-4 spends it fast.** The J79s are thirsty at military power and ruinous in afterburner; the airplane bleeds energy in a turn and buys it back slowly. *Turning the Phantom*, in the systems phase, exists because every new Phantom pilot tries to fight it like a Viper once.

**The weapons were never as good as the brochure.** A Sparrow needs a lock held to impact, a Sidewinder needs a tone and the right aspect, and the gun needs a stable platform and a short range. Treat every shot as something to be set up, not hoped for.

**The threat it feared most was on the ground.** Everything in the weapons phase — low altitude, minimum exposure, one pass, frag clearance by the clock — was learned from SA-6 and ZSU crews who were good at their jobs.
